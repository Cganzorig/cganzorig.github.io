Project note

Why I am looking at SAEs for geospatial foundation models

6 Jun 2026 · Sparse autoencoders · Remote sensing

I started this because geospatial foundation models are powerful, but their internal representations are hard to inspect. A model can perform well on a benchmark while still learning shortcuts tied to brightness, clouds, geography, seasonality, sensor artifacts, or land-cover imbalance.

Sparse autoencoders are interesting here because they give a concrete way to break dense model activations into smaller candidate features. Instead of only asking whether the model output is correct, I can ask what kinds of patterns inside the satellite imagery are being represented.

Why SAEs for geospatial models

Remote-sensing models see more than RGB images. They use multi-band signals such as NIR and SWIR, and the same visible scene can mean different things depending on season, cloud cover, geography, and sensor conditions. That makes ordinary visual inspection incomplete.

SAEs give me a debugging handle. If an SAE feature consistently activates on a visual or spectral pattern, I can inspect the top examples, compare them to random patches, and test whether removing that feature changes the representation. That is closer to a usable model-debugging workflow than a single saliency map.

Issues I ran into

The first issue was that the feature examples were easy to over-interpret. A contact sheet can look coherent, but that does not mean the feature is causally important. I fixed this by adding a simple ablation check: remove the selected SAE feature and compare the representation change against random active features on the same tokens.

The second issue was that RGB-only views are not enough for satellite data. Some features looked like ordinary bright patches, but the stronger clue was in SWIR brightness or texture. I fixed this by summarizing top patches with basic remote-sensing statistics instead of relying only on screenshots.

The third issue was choosing a claim that was not too strong. I do not want to say an SAE feature is a human concept just because it has clean-looking examples. The safer claim is that some features are coherent candidates for interpretation and have measurable effects when ablated.

What I would do next

The next step is to connect these SAE features to downstream geospatial tasks, not just reconstruction-space changes. If a feature matters for land-cover, disaster, or environmental monitoring behavior, then it becomes a much more useful debugging target.

I also want geography-aware baselines. A feature that looks meaningful may simply be picking up region, season, or sensor distribution. Better baselines would help separate real model structure from dataset artifacts.
