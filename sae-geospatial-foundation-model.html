Sparse Autoencoders for Geospatial Foundation Model Interpretability

6 Jun 2026 · Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M · Sparse autoencoders · Remote sensing

I trained a Top-K sparse autoencoder on internal Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M representations and used it to find localized, measurable directions in a geospatial foundation model. The practical question was simple: can SAE features do more than produce nice-looking contact sheets?

The answer from this first pass is yes, with caveats. Several selected SAE features were visually coherent in their top-activating image patches and produced larger reconstruction changes than random active-feature ablations on the same tokens. That makes them useful candidates for actionable interpretability work, not just descriptive labeling.

Experimental Setup

I analyzed Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M, focusing on activations from encoder.blocks.6. The dataset was 500 HLS chips with shape [4, 6, 256, 256], covering Blue, Green, Red, NIR, SWIR1, and SWIR2 bands.

The sparse autoencoder was a Top-K SAE with d_hidden=4096 and k=64. After training, I inspected high-activating patches, computed simple remote-sensing statistics for top patches versus random patches, and ablated individual SAE features inside the reconstruction space.

What The SAE Found

The four main features I inspected were 4093, 3421, 905, and 2460. Their top-activating patches were not arbitrary. They lined up with compact visual and spectral patterns such as bright SWIR/RGB regions, high-edge texture, low-brightness texture, and very bright low-texture regions.

3421 High-edge textured patches 0.0106 4.11

2460 Very bright low-texture patches 0.0011 1.59

Looking In Full-Image Context

Contact sheets are helpful, but they can hide whether a feature is actually localized within the broader chip. I used RGB overlay figures to show each selected top patch in its full-image context.

Ablation Test

The key test was whether selected features caused larger representation changes than random active SAE features. For each target, I compared the selected-feature ablation against random active-feature ablations in the same tokens.

Feature Selected L2 Random L2 Ratio Selected relative change
4093 15.48 2.76 5.60 0.271
3421 14.23 3.46 4.11 0.226
905 7.52 3.35 2.25 0.120
2460 5.14 3.24 1.59 0.091

This is the result that makes the features more interesting than visual clusters. Feature 4093 produced a reconstruction-space change 5.60 times larger than the random baseline. Feature 3421 was also strong at 4.11 times random. Features 905 and 2460 were smaller, but still above baseline.

Why This Matters

Geospatial foundation models are increasingly useful in remote-sensing workflows, but their internal representations are still hard to reason about. Sparse autoencoders give us a way to turn dense activations into a set of candidate directions that can be inspected, summarized, and intervened on.

The actionable part is the intervention loop: find a feature, describe it using top patches and domain statistics, then ablate it and measure whether the representation actually changes more than a comparable random feature. That is a stronger workflow than labeling features by visual inspection alone.

Limitations And Next Steps

This analysis measures changes in SAE reconstruction space, not downstream task behavior. The feature interpretations are preliminary and should be validated with domain expertise. Simple spectral summaries can also miss seasonal, geographic, sensor, and land-cover confounds.

The next steps are downstream task ablations, bootstrapped confidence intervals, a larger feature sweep, and geography-aware stratified baselines. Those would make the causal claims stronger and help separate genuinely semantic directions from dataset artifacts.
